QP SPOTLIGHT — EMBEDDED MPC

Real-time optimization where everyone says it doesn't fit.

The capability I'm most confident in — and the one that separates controls engineers who read about MPC from those who deploy it.

Model predictive control earns its reputation in papers with desktop CPUs and 100-watt power budgets. The interesting engineering starts when the target is a Cortex-M-class microcontroller: kilobytes of RAM, no FPU luxury, hard deadlines shared with safety monitors and comms — and a BOM owner who will delete your feature over a fifty-cent part.

I work at the solver level to make it fit: condensed problem formulations, warm starts, fixed-iteration first-order methods (ADMM-family, in the spirit of OSQP / TinyMPC), structure exploitation, and code generation — so worst-case execution time is provable, memory is static, and the loop never misses. At SharkNinja I've explored and deployed optimization-based model inference strategies for real-time use on exactly this class of silicon; in research at GRASP I built robust, hybrid, and explicit MPC around learned dynamics models.

Why it matters for robotics: actuators saturate, batteries brown out, thermal limits bite — constraints are the product. A controller that treats them as first-class citizens, on the cheapest chip that closes the loop, is how advanced control gets into humanoids, wearables, and every robot that has to hit a price point.

ŷ(t+N) PREDICTION HORIZON — CONTROLS IN 2028

AI is rewriting this job. Here's my read — and my receipts.

A practitioner's forecast of what controls engineering looks like two years out, and the evidence that I'm already working that way.

01

Learned policies on top, guarantees underneath

RL and vision-language-action policies will increasingly decide what robots do — but they'll sit above optimization layers that decide what's allowed: MPC tracking controllers, safety filters, constraint projection. The controls engineer's center of gravity moves to the boundary between learned and guaranteed. Whole-body control stays an optimization problem.

My receiptsBuilt robust / hybrid / explicit MPC around learned dynamics with conformal-prediction safety bounds at GRASP; deployed learning-based control with uncertainty modeling on Verdant's field robots.

02

Models stop being handwritten

System ID becomes continuous and hybrid: physics structure plus data — PINNs, neural ODEs, differentiable simulation — refreshed by telemetry from every deployed unit. The fleet becomes the experiment, and sim-to-real becomes a pipeline rather than a paper problem.

My receiptsPINN / DeepONet / FNO dynamics learning; NARX and neural-ODE models on production robots; day-job experience with consumer fleets as a data source.

03

Solvers and networks shrink to the edge

Embedded MPC (TinyMPC / OSQP-class) and quantized inference (TinyML) put optimization and learned models on microcontrollers that cost less than a coffee. That's what unlocks advanced control in mass-market robots, wearables, and appliances — products where the BOM is the boss.

My receiptsDeploying optimization-based real-time inference on Cortex-M-class silicon in production R&D today; CMSIS / TinyML on the resume, not the wishlist.

04

AI agents multiply the engineer

LLM agents already draft control code, reverse-engineer legacy firmware into specs, generate HIL test suites, and triage fleet logs. The role shifts toward architect and reviewer: the engineer who can specify the problem precisely — costs, constraints, interfaces, failure modes — and verify what the machine writes. Taste for correctness becomes the bottleneck skill.

My receiptsI run this workflow daily — agentic spec extraction from firmware, AI-drafted state-machine documentation, generated test harnesses — reviewed like any junior engineer's PR, with the physics as the referee.

05

What doesn't change

Actuators saturate. Sensors lie. Heat flows where it wants. Safety cases must close before ship dates. No model — learned or otherwise — beats an engineer with a scope probe and a hypothesis when unit #2,000,001 misbehaves. Formulating the right problem and debugging real hardware stay stubbornly human.

My receiptsTwo years of production escalations, HIL fault injection, CAN/latency forensics, and factory realities across four shipping product lines.

What this means if you're hiring

You don't need ten controls engineers who each do one layer. You need a few who can formulate the optimization, wrap the learned model safely, fit it on the target silicon, prove it on HIL, and use AI tooling to move at 5× — then stand behind the safety case. That hybrid profile is what I've been building deliberately, and it's what I'll bring to an Engineer II / Senior controls role.
